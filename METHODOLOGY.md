# Tax-benefit cliffs under 2017 federal rules: methodology

PolicyEngine, November 2026

## What we estimate

An adult faces a cliff when their household's net income would fall if they earned $1,000 more in a year. We count three things nationally:

1. the adults who face a cliff;
2. the households they live in;
3. everyone who lives in those households, children included.

We run the count under 2017 federal tax and benefit rules, with the later values we list under "2017 rules" below, and under 2025 rules for comparison. Each year has two versions: one counting cash and near-cash income only, and one that also counts health coverage (Medicaid, CHIP, ACA premium tax credits, Medicare Savings Programs and state health programs).

The scripts in this repository write the results table, [results/summary.md](results/summary.md), so every number in it traces to a run we describe here.

## How the model finds a cliff

We use PolicyEngine-US, an open-source microsimulation model of federal and state taxes and benefits ([github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us)). The model computes each household's net income, then runs a second version of the household with one adult's earnings $1,000 higher and computes it again. If net income falls, that adult faces a cliff, and the drop is their cliff gap.

- **Who it tests.** The model ranks a household's adults by market income and tests the top two, one at a time. It doesn't test children or a third adult, so it counts at most two adults per household as facing a cliff. The model tests the top two even when they have no earnings; our worker counts keep only adults with positive earnings: wages plus self-employment income, including the self-employment income from specified service businesses that the model holds separately.
- **Share of workers.** We divide the adults on a cliff who have earnings by everyone with earnings. That base includes the people with earnings whom the model doesn't test, children and adults below the top two, who can't count as facing a cliff: 23.5 million of the 177.8 million people with earnings in 2017. Among tested workers alone, each share would rise by a factor of about 1.15.
- **How it adds the $1,000.** The model splits the raise by the adult's current mix of wages and self-employment income, and adds it all to wages when they have no positive earnings. The second version keeps every input and recomputes everything that depends on earnings: taxes, credits and benefits.
- **Net income.** Household net income equals market income plus benefits and refundable tax credits, minus taxes before refundable credits. Benefits include Social Security, SSI, SNAP, WIC, free and reduced-price school meals, TANF, unemployment compensation, housing assistance, Head Start and state benefit programs, as the model's `gov.household.household_benefits` parameter lists them. We leave out the Commodity Supplemental Food Program, which the model adds to its list from 2024 (below).
- **Health coverage.** When we count health coverage, we also add Medicaid, CHIP, the ACA premium tax credit, Medicare Savings Programs and state health programs to net income, and subtract CHIP premiums and marketplace premiums net of the premium tax credit. The model values Medicaid at government cost. It spreads each state's Medicaid spending across the state's enrollees in proportion to a cost index. It builds each enrollee's index from the benchmark marketplace premium for their age, or from an equal share of a family-tier premium among the tax unit's enrollees. It values CHIP by dividing each state's spending on its separate CHIP program by the program's enrollment. PolicyEngine-US holds CHIP spending from 2023 and enrollment from 2022, so the 2017 runs value CHIP at those years' levels.
- **Take-up.** Microcosm supplies take-up for SNAP, SSI, TANF, WIC, housing assistance, Head Start, Medicaid, Medicare, the premium tax credit and the EITC. It stores each person's WIC take-up draw as `would_claim_wic`, a name PolicyEngine-US 2.116.0 no longer reads. policyengine.py, the package PolicyEngine runs the model through, sets the model's `takes_up_wic_if_eligible` from that draw for every month ([PolicyEngine/policyengine.py#531](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine.py/pull/531)). [cliffs2017/wic_take_up.py](cliffs2017/wic_take_up.py) does the same for every simulation here, including the copies the model makes to raise each adult's earnings. Microcosm supplies no input for WIC's nutritional risk test, so the model treats everyone who meets WIC's income and category tests as at nutritional risk.
- **Benefits the model gives to everyone eligible.** PolicyEngine-US gives free and reduced-price school meals, CHIP, Medicare Savings Programs and several state health programs to everyone eligible. Where these programs have a take-up input, Microcosm supplies none, so each input defaults to true. State child care subsidies have no take-up input, except California's CalWORKs child care, which follows TANF take-up, so the model pays them to every family it finds eligible. For some state supplements to SSI, such as Massachusetts's, the model doesn't read SSI take-up either. The model counts breakfast and lunch on all 180 school days for every K-12 child in an eligible family. [scripts/take_up_bounds.py](scripts/take_up_bounds.py) counts the cliffs that would disappear if these benefits didn't change with the raise ([results/take_up_bounds.json](results/take_up_bounds.json)). The script puts every state benefit in one group, including state supplements to SSI that follow SSI take-up, so that group's share overstates the cliffs that rest on full take-up. Under 2017 rules, 51% of the cliffs without health coverage would disappear (school meals 44%, state benefits 7%), and 20% of those in the runs counting health coverage; under 2025 rules, 18% and 11%. If some families don't take up these benefits, only some of those cliffs would disappear, so these shares bound the fall from above. When we count health coverage, families who don't take up CHIP or a Medicare Savings Program could also face cliffs the model misses. Among people who don't qualify for Medicaid, the model gives CHIP to children in most states and, in some states, to pregnant or postpartum people. It values a Medicare Savings Program at $0 for Medicaid enrollees. So when a raise ends someone's Medicaid, the model can start them on either one, and it enrolls everyone who qualifies. Of the adults facing a cliff in the 2017 runs counting health coverage, 230,000 live in households whose CHIP, net of its premium, rises with the raise, and 110,000 in households whose Medicare Savings Programs rise. The per-person files hold only cliff rows, so we can't count the cliffs the model misses this way.

### Medicaid's state totals

Two of the formulas behind Medicaid's value add up over everyone in a run, not over one household. One gives each state's average cost index, which stands in for an enrollee whose own index is zero; the other adds the cost indices of the state's enrollees, and the model divides the state's spending by that sum. A run over a quarter of the households at full weight therefore finds about a quarter of each state's sum: across our four household groups in 2017, it would value each enrollee at a median of about four times the full-sample amount, and at 2.2 to 11.7 times depending on the state and group. Under our pre-2018 premium tax credit reform (below), the model would also take the sum from the unreformed policy while taking each enrollee's index from the reform.

So we compute both totals for the full sample under each configuration's own policy and hold them fixed in every run ([cliffs2017/medicaid.py](cliffs2017/medicaid.py)). Each household's Medicaid value then depends only on its own records and its state, and Medicaid's value over the full sample equals the spending the model spreads. PolicyEngine-US holds state Medicaid spending for 2020 through 2023 only, so the 2017 runs spread the 2020 totals ($661.5 billion across the states and DC) and the 2025 runs spread the 2023 totals ($880.0 billion).

The model sets each Medicaid value in proportion to its state's spending, so when we scale every state's spending by a factor, each adult's change in Medicaid scales by the same factor. The runs compute the counts at 0.8, 0.9, 1.1 and 1.2 times the spending they spread ([results/summary.md](results/summary.md)). When we value Medicaid at 0.8 times that spending, our 2017 headline count with health coverage falls 1.0%, and at 1.2 times it rises 0.4%. When we count the premium tax credit twice, as the model does (below), the count falls 1.2% at 0.8 times and rises 0.2% at 1.2 times. The 2025 count doesn't change at any of the four factors, whichever way we count the credit. [scripts/counted_once.py](scripts/counted_once.py) computes the counted-once figures ([results/counted_once/](results/counted_once/)): it reruns each household group, stops unless the group reproduces the committed counts, and scales each adult's change in Medicaid by each factor.

### The premium tax credit counts twice

When we count health coverage, PolicyEngine-US adds the premium tax credit to net income and also subtracts the marketplace premium net of that credit. A $1 cut in the credit therefore lowers net income by $2 for an enrollee who pays a premium: $1 less credit and $1 more net premium. [scripts/ptc_household.py](scripts/ptc_household.py) shows one case: a Texas adult aged 40 who earns $30,000 in 2025 loses $94 of credit to a $1,000 raise, and net income rises $615.50 as the model counts it, or $709.50 with the credit counted once ([results/ptc_household.json](results/ptc_household.json)). We report the counts as the model computes them and again with the credit counted once, at its value to the government, the way the model counts Medicaid and CHIP. In our headline counts with health coverage, we count the credit once.

### The Commodity Supplemental Food Program

The Commodity Supplemental Food Program (CSFP) gives food packages to people aged 60 or older with low incomes. From 2024, PolicyEngine-US counts it in household benefits and gives a package to every person its eligibility rules admit, with no take-up rate. Weighting up a quarter of the households, we find that the model gives 8.4 million people a package in 2025, worth $5.0 billion in all. The model values each 2025 package at $601, the program's $425 million cost divided by its 707,000 participants ([scripts/csfp_reach.py](scripts/csfp_reach.py), [results/csfp/reach_2025.json](results/csfp/reach_2025.json)). A 2017 run reads the model's benefit list from before 2024, which has no CSFP. So that both years count the same benefits, the 2025 runs leave CSFP out as well ([cliffs2017/benefits.py](cliffs2017/benefits.py)). When we count it as the model does, the 2025 count without health coverage rises by 166,000 adults, or 24%; [results/summary.md](results/summary.md) gives both versions.

### What lowers net income

For each adult on a cliff we break the change in net income into its parts (earnings, each benefit, refundable credits, taxes and health costs) and name the part that falls most. The parts add up to the model's own change in net income for every adult the model tests. Each result file reports `decomposition_flag_mismatches`, the number of adults whose cliff flag the breakdown fails to reproduce.

[scripts/health_only.py](scripts/health_only.py) compares the two headline counts adult by adult ([results/health_only.json](results/health_only.json)). Under 2017 rules, the count with health coverage includes every adult in the count without it, and 1,987,727 of its 2.68 million adults (74%) face a cliff only once we count health coverage. For each of them, net income outside health coverage rises with the raise, by a weighted median of $725, and the value of the household's health coverage falls by more, a weighted median of $7,777. Under 2025 rules, 78% of the adults in the count with health coverage face a cliff only once we count it.

## Other changes in earnings

The model tests each household's two highest-income adults with one change, a $1,000 raise, so it finds only the cliffs that a $1,000 raise reaches. Under 2017 rules, a tax unit that gets the premium tax credit with income $3,000 below 400% of the poverty line keeps the credit when one of its wage earners earns $1,000 more, but crosses the line and loses all of it when they earn $5,000 more. To see how much the count depends on the size of the change, [scripts/earnings_changes.py](scripts/earnings_changes.py) repeats the test with raises of $1,000, $2,000 and $5,000 and of 1% and 10% of each adult's earnings, and with a cut of each size.

- **Raises and cuts.** Under a raise, an adult counts when their household's net income falls. Under a cut, an adult counts when their household's net income rises, so the household would gain if the adult earned less. Both mean net income moves against earnings, and [cliffs2017/changes.py](cliffs2017/changes.py) applies that one test to both. For each size, we also count the adults on either side: those whose household would lose from the raise or gain from the cut, each adult once. We also compare the raise's count with the cut's, as counted and with the raise counted only among the adults the cut reaches, with intervals from the same resamples.
- **Who each change reaches.** A raise in dollars goes to both tested adults, with or without earnings, as the model's own raise does. A cut in dollars goes only to tested adults whose earnings reach it, and a change in proportion to earnings only to tested adults with earnings, so no change takes more of any kind of earnings than the adult has, beyond rounding. Each change splits across wages and self-employment income in the adult's current mix, as the model's raise does. Cuts and changes in proportion to earnings therefore reach fewer adults than raises in dollars, so we give each count as a share of all workers and as a share of the adults the change reaches.
- **One run gives both counts.** Each run counts health coverage, with the premium tax credit counted once, and gets the change in net income without health coverage by adding up every other part of the change. For the $1,000 raise, each part must reproduce both committed counts, the run without health coverage and the health run's count with the credit counted once, in adults, workers, households and members, or the script stops. On a small sample, for five other changes in both years, the tests check that each adult's change in net income outside health coverage comes within a dollar of a separate run without health coverage, and that the same adults count wherever the change exceeds a dollar.
- **Crossing 400% of the poverty line.** For every adult a change reaches, each run records their tax unit's income as a share of the poverty line, as the premium tax credit measures it (`aca_magi_fraction`), and the tax unit's credit, before and after the change. [scripts/earnings_changes_summary.py](scripts/earnings_changes_summary.py) counts the adults whose tax unit crosses 400% of the poverty line; among them, those whose tax unit's credit ends (under a raise) or starts (under a cut); those on a cliff counting health coverage; and those for whom the credit moves net income most against the change. Under 2025 rules the line ends no credit: from 2021 through 2025 the model's `gov.aca.ptc_income_eligibility` admits incomes above it, and `aca_required_contribution_percentage` holds the required contribution at 8.5% of income there. A credit that a raise ends for these adults in 2025 ends because 8.5% of the tax unit's income reaches its benchmark premium.
- **SNAP's rounding.** PolicyEngine-US rounds a household's monthly SNAP net income to whole dollars and its expected contribution up to whole dollars, so a small change in earnings can move SNAP by a dollar a month, $12 a year, from rounding alone. A change of a few dollars, such as 1% of a small wage, mostly tests that rounding, so we also count the adults whose net income moves $12 or less.
- **Intervals.** Each count's 95% interval comes from 5,000 resamples of survey households, as for the counts by largest loss (see "Uncertainty"). The counts share households, so two counts' intervals can't show whether they differ; for each change we also give its difference from the $1,000 raise, with an interval from the same resamples.

[results/earnings_changes.md](results/earnings_changes.md) gives the counts.

## The population

We use Microcosm, PolicyEngine's microdata for the US, in its 2024 build (`policyengine/populace-us`, file `populace_us_2024.h5`, revision `populace-us-2024-spm-20260909`). Microcosm builds on the Census Bureau's Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement, pooling its 2023, 2024 and 2025 surveys, which cover incomes in 2022 to 2024, into 57,240 household records. It adds imputations from the IRS Statistics of Income Public Use File, the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, the Survey of Income and Program Participation, the CPS Outgoing Rotation Groups, the MEPS Insurance Component and the American Community Survey, then calibrates household weights to IRS, Census and program totals. The file holds 9,963 survey households twice, in an `asec` record and a `puf_tax_detail` record that share a `household_source_id`, each with its own weight.

PolicyEngine-US can age a dataset forward but not back, so we build the 2017 inputs ourselves. We scale every dollar amount in the 2024 data to 2017 levels with the index that fits it:

| Inputs | Index | 2017 ÷ 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Wages, self-employment income, tips and wage-linked benefits (unemployment and workers' compensation, disability benefits) | SSA national average wage index | 0.7205 |
| Social Security and veterans' benefits | Cumulative Social Security cost-of-living adjustments | 0.7800 |
| Health insurance premiums people pay, other than Medicare Part B (`health_insurance_premiums_without_medicare_part_b`, `other_health_insurance_premiums`) | CMS employer premium per enrollee | 0.6875 |
| Health insurance premiums people pay, as a single total (`health_insurance_premiums`) | CMS direct-purchase premium per enrollee | 0.7111 |
| Out-of-pocket medical spending (`other_medical_expenses`, `over_the_counter_health_expenses`) | CMS out-of-pocket spending per person | 0.7212 |
| Every other dollar amount | CPI-U, January 2017 over January 2024 | 0.7839 |
| Household weights | Census population | 0.9556 |

We take each index from the model's own parameters and leave ages, family structure and the model's other non-dollar inputs as they were. The 2017 run therefore applies 2017 rules to the 2024 population, with incomes and prices at 2017 levels and weights summing to the 2017 population. It doesn't capture how the population's makeup, its income distribution beyond these indices, or program take-up differed in 2017. We could capture those differences in a larger project, by building a true 2017 population from the 2018 Current Population Survey, which asks about 2017 incomes.

The 2025 runs use the same Microcosm build, which PolicyEngine-US ages forward to 2025 with its own indices.

## 2017 rules

The model holds each tax and benefit parameter with the date its values start, and a 2017 run reads the value in force in 2017 wherever the model holds one. Three gaps matter:

- **The ACA premium tax credit before 2018.** PolicyEngine-US returns no premium tax credit before 2018, because its benchmark premium data start in 2018. Our `ACABefore2018` reform ([cliffs2017/aca2017.py](cliffs2017/aca2017.py)) applies the model's 2018 formula to 2017 with three changes. It sets the 2017 applicable percentages from IRS Revenue Procedure 2016-24 (2.04% of income below 133% of the poverty line, up to 9.69% from 300% to 400%). It scales the 2018 benchmark premiums back with the model's own premium index (359 in 2017, 481 in 2018). And it rates premiums by age with the curves CMS published for 2014 to 2017. The federal default rated everyone aged 0 to 20 at 0.635 of a 21-year-old's premium, while the model's 2018 curve, which it uses for every year, rates ages 0 to 14 at 0.765 and ages 15 to 20 at 0.833 to 0.970. New Jersey also had its own curve then. Medicaid's cost index uses the same age curves, so the reform also changes how the 2017 runs spread Medicaid spending. The model already ends eligibility at 400% of the poverty line before 2021, which reproduces the 2017 subsidy cliff. It truncates income to whole percentage points of the poverty line and denies the credit at a truncated 400%. So it denies the credit at exactly 400%, where section 36B(c)(1)(A) allows it, and agrees with the statute at every other income. We haven't checked how taxpayers rounded income on the 2017 Form 8962, so we don't know whether those from 400% to 401% of the poverty line got the credit. When we count the credit once, losing it lowers net income most for 59,778 of the adults facing a cliff under 2017 rules: 28 sample adults in 17 survey households, two of which supply half the count. Its 95% interval runs from 8,000 to 130,000 ([scripts/driver_intervals.py](scripts/driver_intervals.py)). For 59,776 of those adults, the raise lifts their tax unit's income from below 400% of the poverty line to 400% or more and ends the unit's whole credit ([scripts/counted_once.py](scripts/counted_once.py), [results/counted_once/once_2017.json](results/counted_once/once_2017.json)).
- **Parameters that start after 2017.** Where a parameter's first value postdates 2017, the model uses the earliest value it has, unchanged and in that year's dollars, back to 2015. [results/parameters_read_2017.csv](results/parameters_read_2017.csv) lists every parameter that a 2017 run with health coverage reads on a sample of 40 households from each state, and when each one's values start. policyengine-core skips a formula, and every parameter it reads, when no one in the run meets its `defined_for` condition, so a run on the full data can read parameters this list lacks. Of these, 4,155 come from the model's parameter files. The other 4,074 don't appear in those files, and all but one of them sit under `gov.abolitions.*`. [scripts/audit_parameters.py](scripts/audit_parameters.py) records them with policyengine-core's parameter tracing, which it extends to two kinds of read the tracing misses, bracket scales and reads that formulas write as `parameters.gov.X(instant)`, and [scripts/summarize_parameters.py](scripts/summarize_parameters.py) prints the counts below. For 2,426 of the 4,155, at least one part, such as one state's or one bracket's, has no value in force on 1 January 2017, so the 2017 runs read a later value there. They include 2,167 of the 3,336 state parameters, 48 of the 61 local ones, 208 of the 752 federal ones (121 of them from 2018) and 3 of the 6 calibration and simulation parameters. Those three hold CHIP's spending and enrollment (above) and Medicaid's spending. State programs account for most of the 2,426: 1,080 of the 1,571 state tax parameters start later, 841 of them in 2021, and so do 491 of the 536 state child care subsidy parameters. For example, New Mexico's EITC takes the minimum age of 18 that the state set for workers without children from 2021. SNAP's state utility allowances by household size start in October 2017, the first month of fiscal 2018. Every state's Medicaid and CHIP income limits start in 2018 in the model, whose adult limits cite KFF's January 2018 table, so the 2017 runs apply those. Under the 2018 values, the nine states whose Medicaid expansion took effect later (Idaho, Maine, Missouri, Nebraska, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Utah and Virginia) cover no adults through the expansion group. No state's expansion took effect during 2017: Louisiana's did on 1 July 2016, and Virginia's and Maine's followed in January 2019 ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/state-indicator/state-activity-around-expanding-medicaid-under-the-affordable-care-act/)). WIC's food package values start in 2018 as well, so the 2017 runs read them in 2018 dollars. The age curves start in 2018 as well; our reform replaces them for 2017. A few formulas also read a fixed later date. Oklahoma's EITC uses the federal credit's rules as of 2020, and Virginia TANF uses its need and grant standards as of October 2020. SNAP's time limit for adults without dependents follows the rules in force in June 2025: it covers ages 18 to 54, where the 2017 rule stopped at 49, and it exempts veterans, homeless people and former foster youth, which the 2017 rule didn't. Microcosm has no inputs for homelessness or foster care, so those two exemptions never apply.
- **Formulas that start or change after 2017.** Before its first formula takes effect, a variable has no formula, and the model returns 0 for it unless the data supply it. [results/formula_starts_2017.csv](results/formula_starts_2017.csv) lists the 29 variables whose first formula starts after 2017, among them Maryland's senior tax credit and child tax credit, and the model's Medicaid rules for long-term care, which start in 2026, so the 2025 runs lack them too ([scripts/audit_formula_starts.py](scripts/audit_formula_starts.py)). [results/formula_changes_2017.csv](results/formula_changes_2017.csv) lists the 14 variables whose formula changes after 2017 ([scripts/audit_formula_changes.py](scripts/audit_formula_changes.py)). In 2017, four of them return a constant placeholder: the premium tax credit and the credit before its filing condition, which our reform replaces, New York's inflation refund, and the Basic Health Program. The Basic Health Program's formula starts in 2026, so the 2025 runs return the placeholder too.

Two programs barely register in either year: Head Start totals $0, and housing assistance $5.5 billion in 2017 and $5.7 billion in 2025, while HUD spent $20.6 billion on housing vouchers alone in fiscal 2017 and $38.3 billion in fiscal 2025 (outlays, [USAspending](https://www.usaspending.gov/federal_account/086-0302)). The counts can miss cliffs from either program. We haven't compared the 2017 runs' other totals with 2017 administrative data.

## Uncertainty

Each headline count comes with a 95% interval: the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors from a bootstrap of 200 resamples of the survey's households, with the same weights. A resample draws every record of a survey household together, as one source household. With 200 resamples, each standard error carries Monte Carlo error of about 5%. The intervals cover sampling error only, not error in the model or the data, and they hold Medicaid's state totals fixed. They also ignore the CPS's design (its strata and clusters) and the overlap between the pooled surveys, which can interview the same household in consecutive years, so they likely understate sampling error. The rows of the results table share households, so the intervals don't tell whether two rows differ.

For the counts by largest loss, [scripts/driver_intervals.py](scripts/driver_intervals.py) resamples all 47,277 of Microcosm's survey households 5,000 times, drawing a household's two records together where the file holds it twice, and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of each count. It computes each count's share of the total within a resample, from the same households, and takes the same percentiles. We use percentiles because a count that few households carry can have a skewed distribution. It also reports how many sample adults and survey households carry each count, and how many of those households supply half of it. Half of each headline count without health coverage comes from 14 survey households in 2017 and 16 in 2025. Once we count health coverage, half comes from 56 and 61 ([results/driver_intervals.json](results/driver_intervals.json), [results/summary.md](results/summary.md)).

## Running it

The full sample needs more memory than one run can use, so we split the source households into four disjoint groups, run each at full weight and add the results. We found one way a household's results depend on other households in the model, Medicaid's state totals; once we hold those at their full-sample values, the four parts add up to a single full-sample run. [tests/test_additivity.py](tests/test_additivity.py) checks that on a small sample in every configuration: when it splits the sample in two, each person's marginal tax rate, net income and cliff flag match the unsplit run. The combine step checks that Medicaid's value over the four parts matches the spending it spreads, that every part ran the same code with the same settings, and that the first part's run without health coverage matches the run over the same households with four times their weights, which would catch results that depend on the weights' scale.

```bash
uv venv --python 3.13 && uv pip install -e ".[test]" && source .venv/bin/activate
scripts/medicaid_totals.sh 4 0   # Medicaid's full-sample state totals, for the health runs
scripts/run_final.sh 4 0 0,1     # parts 0 and 1
scripts/run_final.sh 4 0 2,3     # parts 2 and 3
CSFP=keep scripts/run_final.sh 4 0 0,1,2,3 2025-plain,2025-health   # 2025 counting CSFP
python scripts/csfp_reach.py --year 2025 --fraction 0.25 --out results/csfp/reach_2025.json
scripts/run_batch.sh 0.25 0 && CSFP=keep scripts/run_batch.sh 0.25 0   # quarter-sample runs for the check
scripts/combine_final.sh 4 0     # results/final_*.json, results/csfp/ and results/summary.md
scripts/run_counted_once.sh 4 0 0,1 && scripts/run_counted_once.sh 4 0 2,3   # results/counted_once/
python scripts/take_up_bounds.py # results/take_up_bounds.json
python scripts/driver_intervals.py   # results/driver_intervals.json
python scripts/health_only.py        # results/health_only.json
scripts/run_earnings_changes.sh 2017,2025 0,1 && scripts/run_earnings_changes.sh 2017,2025 2,3   # results/earnings_changes/parts/
python scripts/earnings_changes_summary.py   # results/earnings_changes.json and .md
python scripts/render_results.py results/final_*.json --csfp results/csfp/final_*.json > results/summary.md
python scripts/ptc_household.py  # results/ptc_household.json
python scripts/audit_parameters.py 2017 40 > results/parameters_read_2017.csv
python scripts/summarize_parameters.py results/parameters_read_2017.csv 2017
```

`pytest` runs the tests. They cover the counts, with exact values on a hand-built example and the bootstrap's clusters, and the household split, with the check that one part scales to its subsample run. They also cover the deflation, the pre-2018 credit and its age curves, the code that leaves out CSFP, and the parameter audit's tracing, which must record the reads core's tracing misses and change no value. For Medicaid's totals, they check that the sums add up across parts, follow the model's own formulas, never call the totals they replace, leave each household's value unchanged whichever part holds it, and spread each state's spending in full. For the breakdown, they check that the parts add up, match a fresh simulation of the raised household, count the premium tax credit once on the Texas household above, name the largest loss, and reproduce the Medicaid scaling. For the counted-once checks, they test the outcomes at the 400% line against the model's own eligibility rule and the counted-once Medicaid scaling against a fresh simulation, and they test the combine's refusals. They also test a North Carolina adult at 395% of the poverty line in 2017 whom the raise lifts past 400%, so the adult's tax unit loses its whole credit. For WIC, they check that the model's WIC take-up matches Microcosm's stored draw in the simulation, in the branches the model makes while it computes the cliff test, and in a copy they build with `create_perturbed_branch`, the function the cliff test uses to raise an adult's earnings. They also check that WIC then equals the full-take-up amount times the draw for every person and month in 2017 and 2025, and that [cliffs2017/wic_take_up.py](cliffs2017/wic_take_up.py) stops with an error when the data list people in a different order from the simulation. For the take-up bounds, they check which cliffs disappear without a benefit's change. For the overlap of the two headline counts, they check that the adults in both counts and the adults in only one add up to each count, and that no adult only in the count with health coverage loses net income outside health coverage. For the intervals by largest loss, they check on a hand-built example that counting the credit once names the credit, not the premium net of it, as the largest loss, and that every resample draws as many survey households as the dataset holds. They also check that the resamples reproduce the counts' bootstrap standard error with the same clusters and seed, and that the script stops unless the per-person rows reproduce each committed count. For other changes in earnings, they check that a $1,000 raise reproduces the breakdown row for row, that the parts add up under raises and cuts in dollars and in proportion to earnings, that each adult's change outside health coverage comes within a dollar of a separate run without health coverage, with the same adults counted wherever it exceeds a dollar, and that a $5,000 cut matches a fresh simulation of the household with lower earnings. They check that no change takes more earnings than the adult has and, for a $5,000 cut on the sample, that no kind of earnings goes below zero, that the test flags a raise and a cut symmetrically, and that the summary counts each adult on either side once, reads one file per change, and stops when the parts ran different code. Each result file records the PolicyEngine-US and policyengine-core versions, the dataset revision, the stored inputs the run maps to the model's current names (`stored_input_renames`), the factors applied, the Medicaid totals file, the repository commit, a hash of the `cliffs2017` code and the run time.

## How these numbers relate to our April 2025 estimate

In April 2025 we estimated that 7.4 million people, all of them adults, would face a cliff in 2025. A bug in the model version we used then inflated that count about ninefold. We explain the bug and give the corrected figures in [CORRECTION.md](CORRECTION.md).
